What the house actually costs each month

Most spreadsheets multiply deductions by a blended marginal rate. This one computes your federal and California tax twice — once owning, once not — and reports the difference. That difference is the refund. It also runs the §280A waterfall if you rent rooms to housemates. Every figure shows the formula behind it and why it is written that way.

What was wrong in the old sheet

Two of these pulled hard in opposite directions and nearly cancelled at the original inputs, which is why the old number looked plausible. Change any input and they stop cancelling.

Material

The standard deduction was counted twice

The net-payment line subtracted the gross benefits, not the delta the sheet itself computed one row above. Deducting mortgage interest only helps to the extent it beats the deduction you would get for free.

outlay − rent − gross benefits → tax without the house − tax with it
Material

The SALT cap is $40,400, not $10,000 — and it is shared

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act raised the cap for 2025–2029. But the cap covers property tax and state income tax together, and California income tax fills most of it before property tax gets a turn. The old sheet gave property tax a clean $10,000 of headroom it never had.

rate × MIN(house tax, 10000) → MIN(property tax + CA income tax, cap)
Material

California was never modelled

A single blended rate applied to federally-capped numbers. California allows mortgage interest on $1,000,000 of debt, not $750,000; allows property tax with no cap at all; disallows California income tax entirely; and trims itemized deductions by 6% of AGI over the threshold. None of that survives a blended rate.

one rate, one calculation → two returns, two sets of rules
Material

A flat marginal rate over-credits large deductions

Multiplying by a blended rate assumes every deducted dollar falls in the top bracket. A large deduction walks down through brackets as it goes. This model runs the bracket table twice and takes the difference, which is what the refund actually is.

deduction × blended rate → tax(income − dedA) − tax(income − dedB)
Material

Roommate rent was subtracted before tax

The net-payment line subtracted the full rent. The sheet had a post-tax row that applied (1 − rate) — it just was not wired into anything. And even wired up it would have been wrong, because it taxed the rent without deducting the roommates' share of the costs. The §280A waterfall above does both halves.

an unreferenced post-tax row → Schedule E, tiered, capped at rent
Stale

Standard deduction still read $13,850

That is the 2023 single figure. For 2026 it is $16,100 single, $32,200 joint, $24,150 head of household.

F12 = 13,850 → 16,100 / 32,200 / 24,150
Method

One month of interest, multiplied by twelve

Multiplying month 12's interest by twelve takes it as typical of the whole year. Interest falls every month, so month 12 is below the year's average — and the gap widens the further out you look. This model sums the twelve actual months.

IPMT(month 12) × 12 → Σ interest, months 1–12 of the year
Method

The $750k cap was modelled as a second loan

IPMT(…, −MIN(loan, 750000)) amortizes an imaginary $750,000 loan. Publication 936 asks for the ratio of $750,000 to the average balance of the real loan, applied to the real interest. The two are close in year one and drift apart later.

IPMT on a 750k loan → interest × 750,000 ÷ average balance
Bug

IPMT(…, TRUE) put TRUE in the wrong slot

The fifth argument of IPMT is future value, not the when-due flag. TRUE coerced to a $1 balloon. Harmless at this scale, but it was not doing what it looked like it was doing — and the cell right beside it omitted the argument entirely, so the two interest figures were not computed the same way.

Method

Property tax was frozen at the purchase price forever

Proposition 13 lets the assessed value rise up to 2% a year. Over thirty years that is a 45% increase in the tax bill, and the old sheet showed year 1 for all thirty.

rate × price → rate × price × 1.02year − 1
Method

Cumulative totals multiplied a snapshot by the month count

Multiplying a single month's figure by the month count assumes month 12 repeats. Every cumulative figure here is summed across the schedule instead.

Dead

Six scenario columns, all identical

Six columns labelled "No Roommate" through "2 Roommates", every rent input left at zero, so all six returned an identical number. The two housemate fields on the left do the job.

Check

Insurance and parcel taxes get eyeballed

Two lines people routinely under-enter. California homeowner policies typically run $1,500–$3,000, more on an older building. And the flat school and district charges on a property tax bill are payable but not deductible — they belong in outlay, not in SALT.

Assumptions and limits

What this model assumes

  • Tax years. Federal parameters are 2026 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32 and the OBBBA amendments). California uses the 2025 schedules, the most recent the FTB has published; the 2026 figures index up a few percent.
  • State income tax paid is estimated by running your AGI through the California brackets. If you know your actual withholding, the SALT line is the one it feeds.
  • AGI stays flat in nominal terms across all thirty years, while the property tax assessment grows 2% a year. Real incomes usually rise, which would push the refund up.
  • Average balance for both mortgage caps is the average of the year's opening and closing balance, the Publication 936 method.
  • Comparable rent is flat. No rent inflation, matching the original sheet.

What it deliberately leaves out

  • The allocation percentage. "Their share" is a judgment call, not a fact. Two of three bedrooms is one defensible read; square footage including a per-head split of shared space is another, and they can differ by ten points. Pick a method, write down why, and keep it consistent year to year.
  • Rental-share interest and the $750k cap. This model treats the Schedule E share as a business expense outside §163(h), so the cap only trims the personal share. That is the common reading, not a settled one — worth raising with a preparer before you rely on it.
  • Suspended losses. Carryforwards are shown but not tracked across years; at your income the §469 passive-loss allowance is fully phased out anyway, so they would sit unused either way.
  • AMT, the 3.8% net investment income tax on rental income, and capital gains stacking.
  • Closing costs, points, PMI, and transfer tax. At 20% down there is no PMI, but closing costs are real and one-time.
  • Selling. No agent commission, no transfer tax, no §121 exclusion on the way out.
  • The opportunity cost of the down payment — $240,000 invested elsewhere.
